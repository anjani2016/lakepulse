LAKEPULSE

Lake Trend Detective

Is the lake really changing, or just wiggling? Long NASA records of one lake and its surroundings, put to a trend test.

Hypothesis and observations

Is Lake Simcoe's surface water warming over the open-water season, and what moves with it?

Key result

What to keep in mind

  • The satellite's daytime pass has drifted earlier over the years, so daytime lake temperatures can shift for that reason alone; the night-time series is the safer one.
  • MODIS measures the water's top skin, not the water column.
  • Rain, greenness, haze, NO₂ and MODIS land cover cover a box around the catchment about twice the catchment's area, not the exact catchment.
  • Two land-cover maps disagree; MODIS land cover is not used for the analysis (its guide warns against reading change from it), and the 30 m model's 9-year record is too short to test a trend. About the 30 m model →
  • Weather values come from a computer model, and every value is regional, not a reading at the lake. Links between variables are associations; one lake cannot establish cause.

How the detective works

  1. Gather long records. Monthly values for the lake and the land and air around it, from NASA satellites (MODIS, OMI, GPM) and NASA's MERRA-2 weather model. A series needs at least years before we test it for a trend.
  2. Remove the seasons. Summer is always warmer than winter; that cycle is not a trend. Each year we average the open-water season () into one number.
  3. Measure the rate: Sen's slope. The middle value of the slopes between every pair of years, so one odd year cannot pull it far. The 95% range shows how fast or slow the true trend could plausibly be.
  4. Test it: Mann-Kendall. Do later years beat earlier years more often than chance would allow? We call a trend significant when the p-value is below 0.05.
  5. Compare with the sensor. A change smaller than the instrument's stated accuracy is reported as not distinguishable from it.
  6. Look for what moves together. Correlations between variables, on the raw series and again with each one's straight-line trend removed, against expectations written before the results.
  7. Cross-check satellite against model. Compare the satellite's lake trend with the weather model's air trend, and test whether the satellite's pass time drifted.

Practise first on the made-up Practice Lake: its built-in trend is known, so you can check that the method finds it.

        Why we trained a model

        To ask whether the land around the lake affects it, we need to know how much of the catchment is forest, cropland and built-up. The obvious source, MODIS land cover, maps the land in 500 m cells, and its user guide warns against reading change from it. Runoff enters the lake through a narrow strip along the shoreline and streams, far finer than 500 m.

        So we fine-tuned Prithvi-EO-2.0, an Earth-observation foundation model from IBM, NASA and Jülich Supercomputing Centre, to draw a 30 m land-cover map from Sentinel-2 satellite imagery, and ran it on each summer from 2017 to 2025.

        How its output is used, and its limits

        • Its yearly forest, cropland and built-up shares appear under "The land around the lake" in the explorer.
        • They are model estimates, not measurements. The model learned to reproduce a published map, so its scores measure agreement with that map, not field-checked ground truth.
        • Nine years (2017–2025) is shorter than the -year minimum, so we do not test these shares for a trend.
        • MODIS land cover is shown for comparison only. The two maps disagree substantially even over the same area.

        No other models are trained

        The trend and correlation results use standard statistics (Sen's slope, Mann-Kendall, Pearson correlation), not machine-learning models. Weather values come from MERRA-2, NASA's reanalysis model; we read its output, we do not train it.
